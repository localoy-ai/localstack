# Screenwriting: scenes and dialogue

A script is a plan for what people will see and hear, in order. Write only
what can be seen or heard.

## Structure

- **Three movements**, at any length: set up (who, where, what they want),
  complicate (what stands in the way, and gets worse), resolve (they get
  it, lose it, or change). In a 30 s piece each movement is a few seconds;
  in an episode, minutes.
- **The want and the obstacle** drive every story. If a character wants
  nothing, nothing happens.
- **Turns**: each scene should end in a different place than it began
  (hope → fear, ignorance → knowledge). A scene with no turn is cut.
- **Setup and payoff**: what you show early (the knock, the countdown, the
  photo) comes back later and matters. Don't show what never pays off.

## Scene craft

- **Enter late, leave early.** Start at the moment things are already
  happening; leave before it winds down.
- **One purpose per scene**, and know it: reveal, raise the stakes, show who
  someone is, or move the plot.
- **Write action as pictures**: "Tania's hand shakes on the handle" not
  "Tania is nervous".
- **Concrete over vague**: a specific object, sound or gesture beats an
  adjective.
- **Subtext**: people rarely say what they mean. The tension is in what
  they don't say.

## Dialogue

- **Short lines.** Real people speak in fragments. Read it aloud; cut what
  trips you.
- **Each voice different**: word choice, rhythm, what they notice. Cover
  the names; you should still tell who's speaking.
- **No exposition dumps** ("As you know, Karim, we've been on this station
  for 200 days"). Let facts slip out under pressure.
- **Narration** (voice-over) adds what the picture can't: the inner thought,
  the time jump, the irony. It never describes what we already see.
- **Language**: write in the language it will be spoken in, not translated
  afterwards. For Bangla, natural spoken Bangla (চলিত), not bookish; keep
  English words where people really use them.

## Pace and length

- Spoken English runs about 2.5 words a second at a calm pace; drama and
  narration slower, ads faster. Time every line.
- Silence is a line too: a held beat after a reveal is often the
  strongest moment.
- When it's too long, cut scenes before you cut words inside lines.

## Genre notes

| Genre | What it needs |
|---|---|
| Thriller / mystery | A question early, clues planted fairly, a ticking clock, withheld information |
| Horror | Dread over shocks: sound, darkness, the thing not shown; a rule the audience learns |
| Comedy | Specific details, rule of three, the turn on the last word, timing (a beat before the punchline) |
| Drama | A choice that costs something; restraint |
| Kids | Clear wants, bright stakes, repetition, kindness wins |

## What this means in VertX

- Each script scene becomes one or more timeline pieces: a `picture` or
  `video` for what we see, a `voice` per spoken line, `text` for on-screen
  words.
- A scene's time window is where its pieces go (`at`, `for`); `sync`
  places scenes on the narration's words when the voice is the clock.
- Silence is just a gap in the voice track; keep the picture moving
  (`motion`) through it.
